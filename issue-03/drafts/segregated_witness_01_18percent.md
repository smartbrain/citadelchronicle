# THE SEGREGATED WITNESS
## Column by Don Sambandaraksa • Former Technology Editor, Bangkok Post
### Issue 03 • Block Height 974,102 • October 2026

**Kicker**: MEMOIRS OF THE POST DATABASE • SEPTEMBER 2010
**Headline**: EIGHTEEN PER CENT AND PROUD OF IT
**Subhead**: How a delivery of frozen sausages revived a sixteen-year-old epiphany in Chiang Mai on the true price of capital, the cruelty of interest rate caps, and the moral hazard of the nanny state.

This morning, an express van pulled up to my gate to deliver a parcel of frozen sausages dispatched from Ratchaburi. Emblazoned across the side of the vehicle was the green-and-white livery of NIM Express. It is a peculiar facet of human memory that an ordinary cardboard box of breakfast meat can instantly dislodge an encounter buried sixteen years in the past.

In September 2010, I was on the technology beat for the *Post Database* at the *Bangkok Post*. IBM had ferried a contingent of tech journalists up to Chiang Mai for what the industry’s polite society terms a “geo-expansion showcase”—what the rest of us recognised as a corporate fluff junket. Big Blue was strutting its northern conquests: 70 terabytes of medical imaging storage for Chiang Mai University, containerised modular data centres on flatbed trucks, and Lotus Live collaboration suites.

Tucked into the briefing pack as a star regional commercial showcase was Nim See Seng Leasing. They commanded 233 branches across eight northern provinces, powered by centralised IBM P-Series servers and DB2 databases. Their core business? Providing vehicle and motorcycle loans to low-income, informal-sector borrowers who could never dream of securing an audience with a commercial bank.

The executives presented their metrics with straight faces and unmistakable corporate pride: Nim, they beamed, was performing an uplifting social service by lending at “only” 18 per cent annual interest.

Eighteen per cent! Sitting in that air-conditioned ballroom as a comfortable urban scribe, my jaw hit the teak parquet. Eighteen per cent interest, and they called it cheap? Was this not textbook usurious predation—capitalist financiers extracting blood from the poorest labourers on two wheels?

Then I checked my reporting notes from the provincial streets. The prevailing uncollateralised street rate charged by village loan sharks across the north was 25 per cent. Often higher. Often compounded monthly, and collected under the implicit threat of a lead pipe.

Now that I am sixteen years older—and, one hopes, a fraction wiser—I find myself returning often to that Chiang Mai epiphany. High interest rates are not inherently an evil conspiracy of elites feasting upon the destitute. Rather, interest is the honest, unvarnished market price of risk.

What is “fair”? What is “access”?

If the righteous paternalists in parliament pass an edict capping interest rates out of ostensible compassion, what actually happens? The laws of risk do not bow to ministerial decrees. Two things inevitably follow: either the man at the bottom of the pyramid is denied the motorcycle that allows him to commute, carry produce, and earn his livelihood; or he is driven straight back into the shadows of the loan shark at 25, 30, or 50 per cent.

Are we kind when we cap interest, or are we cruel? In pretending that high-risk capital should be artificially cheap, the state does not protect the vulnerable; it subsidises moral hazard, breeds a sub-class dependent on perpetual debt moratoria and political bailouts, and outlaws the bottom rungs of the formal credit ladder. The market price of risk is uncomfortable. The state’s compassionate illusion is ruinous.
