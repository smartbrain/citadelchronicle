### I. The Smoke from Paris

If you wish to observe the terminal decay of the fiat welfare state, one need only tune one’s receiver to Paris. For weeks, the French capital has resembled an open-air combustion chamber: student strikes, barricades along the boulevards, public schools left without teachers for eight months, and an effective tax burden touching seventy percent—all to finance an unsustainable social apparatus that consumes more than the nation produces.

Listening to my brother, Piriya, discuss the European unrest alongside our father, Lung Chaloke, on the family airwaves of *CDC Talk*, his diagnosis was incisive: the riots are not the product of political awakening, but the blind, volcanic fury of betrayed expectations. When citizens are taught to surrender half their income to a benevolent state in exchange for cradle-to-grave security, and the state inevitably runs out of other people’s money, the social contract does not merely fray; it combusts.

Yet in a delicious twist of historical irony, the failure of French statecraft found an eerie technical parallel this month in France’s most celebrated cryptographic export: a certain Parisian hardware wallet manufacturer.

### II. The Parasite in the Foam

For years, the standard advice dispensed by Bitcoin educators—my brother included—has been impeccably orthodox: remove your capital from centralized custodians, procure a hardware vault, and take custody of your own destiny. 

It is sound counsel, but it has always carried a quiet, stubborn blind spot: the physical journey. In our family debates, I have long played the unrepentant cynic, cautioning Piriya that no amount of mathematical encryption can secure a parcel once it leaves the factory gates and begins its journey through customs warehouses, freight forwarders, and courier vans.

That logistics reality arrived with a vengeance this month. As forensic teardowns by Mark Karpelès have laid bare, an enterprising syndicate intercepted shipments of the Ledger Nano X bound for regional vendors. Without touching the secure element chip, the attackers gently lifted the OLED display, excised the factory foam dampener, and inserted a hair-thin flexible circuit board with an embedded eSIM. 

The mechanism was as brilliant as it was brazen: as the user initialized the device, the parasite board read the twenty-four recovery words directly off the glass display and broadcast them across the cellular network to the thieves. It was, as Piriya candidly observed on air, an ATM skimmer in a velvet box.

Rather than retreating into corporate PR spin or denial, my brother did something vanishingly rare among public educators: he addressed the flaw with unvarnished intellectual honesty. Acknowledging that transit interception is an intractable problem for single-device custody, he noted with a dry, self-deprecating chuckle that his older brother's longstanding warnings about supply-chain vulnerability had finally come due. More importantly, he revised his stance in real-time, urging users toward multi-vendor multisig setups where no single intercepted parcel can compromise the treasury.

### III. The Lifeboat Heresy

This willingness to discard dogma led naturally to an even deeper revelation. For years, Piriya’s educational venture has sold a remarkably popular cotton T-shirt stamped with the ubiquitous rallying cry: *“Fix the Money, Fix the World.”*

Live before his listeners, however, he confessed that he had grown thoroughly disillusioned with his own slogan. 

Promising that an incorruptible ledger will miraculously abolish human greed, eliminate war, and repair fallen institutions is the kind of naive utopianism that sets men up for heartbreak. His working revision is infinitely more grounded: *“There is no need to fix the world. Fix the money, and it simply becomes a tool that allows you to survive. That is enough.”*

Lung Chaloke anchored the thought in classical Buddhist realism. The mortal plane is inherently imperfect; to imagine that one could eradicate all selfishness from humanity is to play the role of the destroyer. Like Thanos attempting to balance the cosmos with a snap of his fingers, erasing human flaws erases the realm itself. One might as well extinguish the fires of hell—succeed, and the realm vanishes, leaving the King of the Underworld sitting in lonely irrelevance.

As France burns under the weight of its broken socialist promises, that distinction matters. Sound money is not a moral crusade to inaugurate heaven on earth; it is an unadorned wooden lifeboat tossed upon a stormy sea. You do not hold satoshis to save society from itself. You hold them so that when the state devalues the currency, defaults on its pledges, and paves over the floodplains, you and yours possess the means to stay afloat. 

Survive first. Leave the salvation of the world to the devas.
