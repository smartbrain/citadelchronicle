### I. The View from March 2010

Pulling yellowed copy from the *Bangkok Post* morgue is an exercise in humility. On Thursday, 25 March 2010—slated for the following Wednesday's *Post Database* broadsheet—I sat down with Paul Edwards, Managing Director for Thailand at NCR. At the time, the world was still largely married to the magnetic stripe, and NCR—a grand industrial titan that began life in Dayton, Ohio in 1884 as National Cash Register—was busy preaching the gospel of the next automated frontier.

To understand where cash dispensing was headed, Edwards first walked me through where it had crawled from. 

The lineage of the mechanical teller began on 27 June 1967 at a branch of Barclays Bank in Enfield, north London. Manufactured by De La Rue Systems, the press promptly christened the contraption the "robot cashier." It was a primitive beast: the machine dispensed a single, crisp £10 note in exchange for a specialised paper voucher that the mechanism permanently retained. Because the unit possessed no telecommunications link to check account balances, Barclays prudently restricted the facility to an elite roster of high-net-worth gentlemen who could be trusted not to bankrupt the branch after pub hours.

Five years later, in December 1972, Lloyds Bank unveiled the second great leap: the IBM-developed "Cashpoint." It was the first terminal to operate online, the first to issue returnable plastic cards, and the first to demand a personal identification number (PIN). A withdrawal took forty agonizing seconds, capped at £20 in rigid multiples of two-pound notes. 

NCR crashed the party in 1975 with its Model 770. By 1984, the firm made history when an American tourist named Mary Stone inserted her Visa card into an ATM in Sydney, Australia, pulling out Australian dollars from her Arkansas bank balance in eight seconds flat. That same year, NCR unveiled the British-designed Model 5070, which became the best-selling cash dispenser in history and knocked IBM off the automated banking throne for good.

The commercial incentive was brutal arithmetic. In 2010, Edwards pointed out that an over-the-counter transaction with a flesh-and-blood human teller cost an American bank $1.07. The same transaction executed by an ATM cost twenty-seven cents. Shifting foot traffic from marble banking halls to street-corner steel boxes saved the global banking cartel tens of billions of dollars every year.

### II. The Hits, The Misses, and The Invisible Radio

Rereading Edwards's 2010 roadmap from the vantage point of 2026 reveals a fascinating split between what human beings eagerly adopted and what they obstinately refused.

Edwards spoke admiringly of Garanti Bank in Turkey, which had begun allowing customers to text a temporary, one-time PIN to a relative, who could then stroll up to any street ATM and retrieve cash without a plastic card. Today, cardless ATM withdrawals are standard operating procedure across Thailand; nobody under forty bothers carrying a debit card when a mobile banking prompt can unlock the dispenser in five seconds.

Yet the industry's grandest technological bet of 2010 flopped spectacularly: Near Field Communication (NFC). Fifteen years ago, NCR was convinced that customers would tap their mobile handset against an invisible receiver pad on the ATM fascia, establish a short-range radio link, authenticate via their mobile network operator, and pocket their notes without ever touching a keypad. 

It never happened.

Instead, Southeast Asia—and Thailand in particular—bypassed the contactless radio entirely in favour of the unglamorous, two-dimensional Quick Response (QR) code. Why? Human psychology. An invisible radio field is unsettling; it raises the instinctive, paranoid suspicion that a digital pickpocket or a rogue reader hidden behind the plastic casing might skim your account balance without your consent. A QR code, by contrast, is entirely optical, tangible, and deliberate. You can see the pattern. You must actively point your camera lens at it. You confirm the payment on your own screen before a single satoshi moves. What people can visually witness and physically control will always feel inherently safer than unseen radio signals floating through the ether.

### III. The Fifteen-Year Horizon

Between the moment an engineer in an NCR research laboratory in Dundee or Atlanta files a patent and the day a street-corner machine in Pathum Thani actually dispenses bank notes via that method, between ten and fifteen years reliably elapse.

Today, those physical cash bastions are beginning their slow, inevitable retreat as central banks push digital surveillance rails and sovereign bitcoiners retreat into unconfiscatable cold storage. 

Which leaves an intoxicating question for the thoughtful observer: if the cardless rails, biometric prompts, and optical scanners we take for granted on the streets today were merely the laboratory curiosities of 2010... what on earth was NCR prototyping behind closed doors five years ago? What are their skunkworks cooking up on their test benches this afternoon? Are they preparing the hardware architecture for central bank digital currencies with built-in expiration dates? Biometric retina-dispensers that deduct carbon rations with every withdrawal? Or perhaps—just perhaps—scaffolding for a world where sovereign individuals demand machines that settle instantly in satoshis without asking a compliance officer for permission?
