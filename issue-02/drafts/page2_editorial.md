### I. The Eleven-Billion-Baht Puddle

With the muddy monsoon floodwaters grudgingly slinking back into the Gulf of Thailand, Bangkok’s political commentariat has dutifully dusted off its favourite seasonal parlour game: finding a villain at City Hall. The incumbent Governor, Chadchart Sittipunt, finds himself the subject of much theatrical huffing and puffing from rival quarters over a decision to quietly excise two long-touted retention ponds (*kaem ling*) from the municipal master plan: **Bung Khu Bon** in Khlong Sam Wa and **Bung Bang Chan** in Min Buri.

The accusation is delivered with customary solemnity: by shelving these two modest reservoirs, the Governor has supposedly condemned eastern Bangkok to a watery grave.

A calm glance at the arithmetic, however, rather spoils the sport. 

City Hall’s own technical reviews concluded that even if both basins were dug to their limits, their combined capacity would amount to a meagre **500,000 to 700,000 cubic metres** of runoff. To acquire that modest puddle, the municipal purse would have been lightened by an eye-watering **11 billion Baht**—nearly $320 million—chiefly to compensate private landowners at prevailing speculative prices. That works out to near enough 20,000 Baht for the privilege of holding a single cubic metre of stagnant water.

Yet one would be naive to mistake City Hall's sudden thrift for civic virtue. So long as those plots remained under the shadow of compulsory purchase decrees, their market value was neatly suppressed. The moment the bungs were struck off the town plan, prime suburban acreage was promptly liberated for lucrative private housing estates—a sequence of events the kingdom's property magnates, including the firm the Governor once steered as chief executive, understand with practiced intimacy. 

It is the classic Bangkok arrangement: politicians make a pantomime of petty earthworks, developers quietly pocket prime real estate, and the ratepayer is left wading through the brown soup.

### II. The Paving of Nong Ngu Hao

To understand why the delta refuses to dry, one must lift one's eyes from City Hall's municipal borders and look twenty-five kilometres east—to the vast, sun-baked concrete apron of Suvarnabhumi International Airport.

Long before architects and duty-free concessionaires descended upon the plain in 2006, the inhabitants of the lower Chao Phraya called that sprawling low-lying depression by its honest name: *Nong Ngu Hao*—Cobra Swamp. It was not vacant wasteland waiting for a terminal building; it was the great natural water-sponge of the eastern seaboard, fashioned by centuries of silt and flood to catch, pool, and slowly release the annual northern deluge into the sea.

The volumetric geometry of what was buried there is chillingly simple:

* **The Footprint:** The airport compound covers 3,200 hectares—precisely **32,000,000 square metres**.
* **The Seasonal Depth:** In an ordinary rainy season, water pooled across the basin to a depth of between half a metre and a full metre. In historically severe inundations—such as the great flood of 2011—water pooled naturally to two metres or more.

A moment’s arithmetic (`Area × Depth = Volume`) exposes the scale of the missing buffer:

| Seasonal Regime | Pooling Depth | Natural Flood Retention Capacity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Standard Monsoon Year** | 0.5 metres | **16,000,000 m³** (16 million cubic metres) |
| **Heavy Monsoon Inundation** | 1.0 metre | **32,000,000 m³** (32 million cubic metres) |
| **Severe Crisis (e.g. 2011)** | 2.0 metres | **64,000,000 m³** (64 million cubic metres) |

### III. The Sixty-Million-Cubic-Metre Lie

In an unsparing season, the old Cobra Swamp quietly swallowed over **60 million cubic metres** of floodwater. That single natural sponge possessed nearly **one hundred times** the combined storage of the two 11-billion-Baht retention schemes over which politicians are currently trading insults.

Today, that sponge is entombed beneath millions of tons of reinforced concrete and asphalt. To prevent commercial jetliners from sliding into the mud, the airport authorities excavated artificial holding ponds with a total static capacity of barely **7 million cubic metres**—roughly a tenth of what nature provided gratis.

To survive the shortfall, the airport runs a formidable battery of high-voltage pumps day and night, forcibly ejecting millions of cubic metres of water over peripheral dykes and into the surrounding countryside. That displaced water does not evaporate into thin air; it backs up into the canals, suburban lanes, and sodden paddy fields of Samut Prakan, Min Buri, and Pathum Thani.

Hanging a municipal administration for abandoning 700,000 cubic metres while living alongside an artificial 60-million-cubic-metre plug is the supreme achievement of modern Thai planning. Bangkok does not drown because a bureaucrat cancelled a couple of retention ponds; it drowns because we built an international airport in the middle of a swamp and expected the monsoon to read the departures board.
